Almost every **icash vs money mart** comparison online leads with price — and price is the one dimension where Canadian law already settled the argument. Both charge **$14 per $100**, because that is the legal ceiling and licensed lenders sit on it. If you are choosing between them on cost, you are comparing two identical numbers. Here is what actually differs.

![icash vs money mart — using a phone calculator to compare the dollar cost, term and due date of two payday loan offers](https://images.pexels.com/photos/35028998/pexels-photo-35028998.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Quick answer

Since **January 1, 2025**, the federal Criminal Interest Rate Regulations cap a licensed payday loan at **14% of the advance**. Both lenders publish exactly that. The six things that do differ: **channel**, **term structure**, **province coverage**, **what else the company sells you**, **the default path**, and **how the APR is displayed**. Only the first five affect what you repay. The sixth just changes how the identical price looks.

## The price comparison is closed — here is the proof

A study of the British Columbia payday market published in **August 2026** examined the years 2012 to 2019, a period covering three separate reductions in the provincial fee cap, and found that lenders **"priced loans at or very near the cap in each year."** Every time the ceiling moved, the market moved with it and stopped there.

That is the clearest available evidence that this market does not compete on price. So the honest version of any **icash vs money mart** comparison starts by removing price from the table:

| Cost element | iCash | Money Mart |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of borrowing | $14 per $100 | $14 per $100 |
| $500 for 14 days | $70 fee, $570 repaid | $70 fee, $570 repaid |
| Maximum advance | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Maximum term | 62 days | 62 days |

Four rows, no differences. Now the rows that matter.

## icash vs money mart difference 1: the APR gap is a term illusion

This is the single most misread number in the category, and it is worth understanding because it will change how you read every payday advertisement from now on.

Money Mart's own provincial disclosures show a **$300 loan for 14 days at 365% APR** in British Columbia and Saskatchewan — and a **$300 loan for 12 days at 425.83% APR** in Manitoba. Same lender. Same $14 per $100. The APR is 60 points higher purely because the term is **two days shorter**.

APR annualises a flat fee. Shorten the term and the same dollar cost annualises to a bigger number. So when a comparison table shows one lender at 365% and another at 425%, it is almost never telling you one is more expensive — it is telling you one has a shorter term. **Compare the dollar fee and the due date.** Those are the only two numbers that leave your account.

| Funding | Interac e-Transfer | Direct deposit or in-branch |

Money Mart's parent, Momentum Financial Services Group, publishes **420+ branches in North America**, **1,500+ employees** and **1 million customers served last year**. That physical footprint has quietly become more distinctive: in March 2026 the parent of the competing Cash Money brand completed a repositioning that moved its payday business **online only**. In-person payday storefronts are becoming rarer, not more common.

## Difference 3: what else is on the shelf

The widest gap in any icash vs money mart comparison is not the loan at all. iCash sells short-term loans. Money Mart operates as a multi-product cash-services retailer — cheque cashing, currency exchange, prepaid cards, money transfers and instalment loans sit alongside the payday product in the same branch.

That cuts both ways. If you need a cheque cashed today without a bank account, one of these companies can help and the other cannot. But a store with an instalment loan on the shelf can also move a $500 payday problem into a multi-thousand-dollar multi-year obligation in a single visit. Know which product you walked in for.

## Difference 4: province coverage

Each lender is licensed province by province, so coverage is not identical and it changes. iCash publishes provincial pages for **Ontario, British Columbia, Alberta, Manitoba, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island**. Money Mart states payday loans are **not available to residents of Newfoundland and Labrador**. Neither offers a conventional payday loan in **Quebec**, where a 35% annual rate ceiling makes the product unworkable.

Confirm coverage on the lender's own provincial page and in your provincial licence register on the day you apply — not from a comparison table, including this one.

## Which one fits which situation

Because price is off the table, the choice comes down to circumstance. This is the matcher most comparisons never bother to write.

| Your situation | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You bank online and want no branch visit | **iCash** | Application and e-Transfer funding without leaving home |
| You have no bank account, or banking access is unreliable | **Money Mart** | A branch can transact in person |
| You need a cheque cashed, not a loan | **Money Mart** | iCash does not offer that service |
| You want to avoid being offered other products | **iCash** | One product, no counter to walk past |
| You prefer to ask questions of a person | **Money Mart** | Face-to-face service |
| You live outside a branch footprint | **iCash** | Online lending is not limited by store locations |
| You need more than $1,500, or longer than 62 days | **Neither** | That is instalment territory, capped at 35% APR |
| This would be your third short-term loan this quarter | **Neither** | Ask for the extended payment plan and speak to a credit counsellor |

Notice how little of this table is about lender quality. In a market where the regulator has fixed the price, the ceiling, the term limit and your cancellation rights, choosing between these two is mostly a logistics decision — and the genuinely important decision, whether to use this category at all, sits above both brands.

## Difference 5: the default path, where costs really diverge

The $14 is a price for repaying on time. Under the federal regulations, a **dishonoured payment fee** and **default interest** sit *outside* the 14% calculation. In Ontario the ceilings are a **$20** dishonoured payment fee and **2.5% per month**, non-compounding, on outstanding principal. Add your bank's NSF charge — capped at **$10** at federally regulated banks since March 12, 2026 — and one failed debit costs more than any brand difference in this article.

Before signing either agreement, find these three clauses: what happens on a failed payment, whether you can repay early without penalty, and whether the lender may re-present a debit.

## Difference 6: your rights are identical — use them

This is the part of the icash vs money mart question that answers itself. Whichever you pick, the same statutory protections attach: **$14 per $100** maximum, **50% of net income** per loan in Ontario, **two business days** to cancel in Ontario and B.C. (a **48-hour clock** in Manitoba), no rollovers, one loan at a time, and a mandatory **extended payment plan** on a third loan within 63 days in Ontario. These are rights, not concessions. Ask for the extended payment plan by name.

## The bottom line

The honest conclusion to **icash vs money mart** is that the brand is the least important variable. The price is legislated, the APR gap is an artifact of term length, and the real decision is channel preference plus whether the due date fits your pay cycle.

If the amount is above $1,500 or you need longer than 62 days, neither is the right product — a regulated instalment loan capped at 35% APR is a different and far cheaper category. And if this would be your third short-term loan this quarter, the comparison to run is not between two lenders but between borrowing again and calling a non-profit credit counsellor.
