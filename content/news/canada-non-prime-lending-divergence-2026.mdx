Canada non-prime lending produced two contradictory quarters within twenty-four hours this week. On August 5, Propel Holdings reported record revenue, originations up 25% and its twelfth straight dividend increase. On August 6, goeasy reported originations **down 70%**, net income down 83%, and an indefinitely suspended dividend. Same country, same segment, same three months.

## The two quarters side by side

| Measure | **goeasy** (Aug 6) | **Propel Holdings** (Aug 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $390.0M, **−10%** | $179.6M, **+26%** (record) |
| Originations | $272.1M, **−70%** | $243.4M, **+25%** |
| Loan book | $5.00B receivable, −2% | $639.1M CLAB, **+23%** |
| Net charge-offs | **16.7%**, up from 8.7% | **12%**, stable |
| Net income | $15.9M, **−83%** | $16.2M, **+7%** |
| Dividend | **Suspended indefinitely** | **Raised 6%** — 12th straight |

The most arresting line is the fifth. **The two companies earned almost identical net income this quarter** — $15.9 million against $16.2 million — despite goeasy carrying a loan book roughly eight times larger.

## Why Canada non-prime lending split

The divergence across Canada non-prime lending traces to a single variable: **credit losses**.

goeasy's net charge-off rate rose to **16.7% from 8.7%** a year earlier — roughly doubling. Its allowance for credit losses climbed to **$499.5 million**, or **9.99% of gross receivables**. A lender writing off close to a sixth of its book annually has to act, and goeasy did: it cut originations, repaid its **$314.0 million** revolver in full, improved leverage to **4.95x from 5.30x**, and suspended both dividend and buyback.

Propel's net charge-offs stayed at **12% of average balances**, flat year over year, with provisions at 50% of revenue. Stable losses let it keep growing.

Two conclusions follow, and the second is less obvious:

- **This is not simply "the Canadian consumer is deteriorating."** If it were purely macroeconomic, both lenders would show it. One did and one didn't.
- **Mix and underwriting are doing the work.** goeasy named merchant-originated **automotive and powersports** lending as an area it tightened. Propel's fastest growth came from **Lending-as-a-Service (+150%)** and **the UK (+53%)** — lines with different risk profiles from Canadian direct-to-consumer lending.

## Does Canada non-prime lending supply actually shrink?

On balance, probably yes — because of scale.

goeasy's origination decline was roughly **$631.6 million** quarter-over-quarter against the prior year. Propel's increase was roughly **$48.7 million**. Even a strong growth quarter at the challenger replaces a small fraction of what the incumbent withdrew.

That said, these are two listed companies in a market that also includes credit unions, banks, private lenders and many smaller online lenders that do not report publicly. **Two data points are not a market total**, and it would be wrong to extrapolate a national credit-availability figure from them.

What can be said with confidence: **the largest listed non-prime lender in Canada substantially reduced new lending, and its nearest listed competitor did not.**

## Why this matters to borrowers

Four practical consequences of the Canada non-prime lending divergence.

1. **Lender choice matters more than it did.** When competitors' criteria move in opposite directions, where you apply becomes a larger factor in the outcome than small differences in your own file.
2. **A single decline is weaker evidence.** Being turned down by a lender in retrenchment says relatively little about your prospects elsewhere.
3. **Specific products tightened, not everything.** goeasy identified merchant-originated automotive and powersports loans. If that is what you need, expect a harder market.
4. **Spend inquiries carefully anyway.** Applying to several lenders at once still leaves several inquiries. Wider shopping should mean more selective, sequenced applications — not simultaneous ones.

## The macro context around it

The Canada non-prime lending divergence lands in a month of otherwise improving indicators. Canada's July jobs report showed employment up **75,000** and unemployment at **6.4%**, a two-year low, with average hourly wages up **2.8%** — matching the most recent inflation reading.

That combination is worth holding carefully. **A labour market improving in aggregate and a lender writing off 16.7% of its book are not contradictory**: charge-offs reflect loans written twelve to twenty-four months ago, in different conditions. Credit losses are a lagging indicator, which is exactly why they can worsen while employment improves.

## What to watch next in Canada non-prime lending

Three markers will show whether this quarter was a turning point or a blip.

1. **goeasy's Q3 originations.** Guidance implies a flat-to-smaller loan book for 2026. If originations recover meaningfully, the pullback was tactical; if they stay near $272 million, it is structural.
2. **Whether Propel's charge-offs hold at 12%.** Stable losses are what make its growth sustainable. A rise would suggest the two lenders are converging after all, just on a lag.
3. **Whether other lenders follow.** Credit unions, banks and private lenders do not report on this cadence, so the public picture is partial. Watch for tightening signals in products rather than press releases — smaller maximum amounts, shorter terms, more documentation.

## Key takeaways

- **goeasy cut originations 70%** to $272.1M; **Propel grew them 25%** to $243.4M.
- **Charge-offs diverged**: 16.7% (up from 8.7%) versus 12% (stable).
- **goeasy suspended its dividend**; Propel raised its for the twelfth consecutive quarter.
- **Near-identical net income** — $15.9M vs $16.2M — despite an eightfold difference in loan book size.
- **Aggregate supply likely tightened**, since goeasy's withdrawal far exceeds Propel's growth in dollar terms.
- **Credit losses lag the economy**, which is why they can rise while employment improves.

## The bottom line

Canada non-prime lending did not have one quarter in Q2 2026; it had two. The incumbent retrenched hard to protect its balance sheet, and the challenger grew without loosening its credit standards.

For borrowers, the takeaway is neither panic nor complacency. Credit has not vanished — but it has moved, and the gap between what one lender will approve and what another will approve is now unusually wide. That makes deliberate, sequenced applications more valuable than they have been in years.
