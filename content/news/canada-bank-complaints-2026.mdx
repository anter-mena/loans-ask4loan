**Canada bank complaints 2026** reached a turning point early this year, when the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) ordered Bank of Montreal (BMO) to pay a **$4 million penalty** for breaching its disclosure obligations. FCAC found BMO charged erroneous monthly plan fees to **101,091 customers** between 2010 and 2024, and failed to clearly disclose when those fees would start between 2022 and 2024. BMO has since refunded affected customers and redressed interest totalling **more than $3 million**. It is the clearest sign yet that the system behind canada bank complaints 2026 is finally holding banks accountable on fees.

![Canada bank complaints 2026: a customer reviews BMO monthly plan fees on a bank statement before filing a complaint](https://images.pexels.com/photos/4968391/pexels-photo-4968391.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## What FCAC found at BMO

The penalty was not arbitrary. FCAC said the $4 million reflected the degree of BMO's negligence in preventing and detecting the problem — the bank had received **more than 500 customer complaints** about the plan fees before the regulator acted. The discounted plans were originally offered to groups such as newcomers to Canada, medical and dental students, and Indigenous customers, whose branch paperwork carried an incorrect fee-waiver start date. That single disclosure error compounded across 14 years.

| The BMO case at a glance | Detail |
|---|---|
| Penalty ordered by FCAC | **$4 million** |
| Customers financially impacted | **101,091** |
| Erroneous monthly plan fees charged | **2010–2024** |
| Failure to clearly disclose fee start | **2022–2024** |
| Refunds and redressed interest | **More than $3 million** |

This is what modern enforcement looks like: not just a fine, but a requirement to make every affected customer whole. For anyone who has ever squinted at a fee they could not explain, the message is that "the bank charged it, so it must be right" is no longer a safe assumption.

## One national ombudsman now hears the appeals

The BMO case lands in a system that was rebuilt in the customer's favour. Since **November 1, 2024**, the **Ombudsman for Banking Services and Investments (OBSI)** has been the single external complaints body (ECB) for every federally regulated bank in Canada. Before that, banks could pick between competing complaint bodies — a setup critics said let institutions shop for the friendliest referee. Now there is one national ombudsman, and its reviews are independent and free.

Two more rules sharpen the process. Banks must now give you a **written final response within 56 days** of receiving a complaint, so a grievance can no longer be left to drift. And in 2026 FCAC published a **revised guideline on complaint-handling procedures** for banks, tightening how institutions must log, escalate and answer the complaints they receive.

## How Canada Bank Complaints 2026 Get Escalated

Knowing the exact path turns a vague frustration into a process a bank must respond to. If a fee looks wrong, work through these steps in order.

| Step | What you do | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Complain to your bank | Put it in writing, describe the issue and the fix you want, and keep a dated record. | Start here |
| 2. Get the final response | The bank must issue a **written final response**. | Within **56 days** |
| 3. Escalate to OBSI | If it is unresolved or you are unsatisfied, take it to **OBSI** — free and independent. | After 56 days or a final response |
| 4. Report to FCAC | FCAC supervises conduct but does not settle individual disputes; reporting feeds enforcement. | Any time |

The paper trail is your strongest asset. The BMO outcome exists partly because customers flagged the fees — 500-plus complaints — and the framework converted that pressure into refunds.
