**Published August 19, 2026**

## Brief summary

New Bank of Canada research on **private credit Canada** exposure, published in August 2026 by Wendy Chan, Cameron MacDonald and Geneviève Vallée, estimates that Canadian financial institutions and funds hold roughly **$500 billion** of private credit — the large majority of it outside the country. The Bank's assessment is that domestic exposures "appear manageable," while flagging limited transparency as a monitoring concern.

![private credit Canada — a hand holding Canadian currency, representing the domestic exposure behind new Bank of Canada research](https://images.pexels.com/photos/14046229/pexels-photo-14046229.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Introduction

Private credit is the fastest-growing corner of global finance that most people have never heard of, largely because none of it is publicly traded and very little of it is publicly reported.

The Bank of Canada has now put a number on how much of it Canadian institutions hold. The figure is large — and the more interesting finding is where it sits.

## The main story: mapping the private credit Canada holds

The article, part of the Bank's *Sparks at the Bank* series, breaks the exposure down by institution type:

| Holder | Exposure | Share of invested assets |
|---|---|---|
| Large Canadian pension funds | **~$215 billion** (end-2025) | ~**9%** |
| Three largest life insurers | **just over $200 billion** (Q1 2026) | ~**22%** |
| Canadian investment funds | **$54 billion** (2025) | ~1.5% of net assets |
| Banks — loans to asset managers | **at least $40 billion** (Q1 2026) | ~**1%** of overall lending |
| **Total Canadian exposure** | **~$500 billion** (early 2026) | — |

Two details stand out beyond the headline total.

The **investment fund** slice is the fastest-growing, up **more than 60% since 2020**, with **over two-fifths** tied to real estate. And the **life insurer** concentration is the highest of any group: just over $200 billion represents roughly **22% of the three largest insurers' invested assets**, against 9% for pension funds.

### Where private credit Canada exposure actually sits

The Bank is explicit that the bulk of this activity is abroad, primarily in the United States. This is Canadian institutional money lent into foreign markets rather than a domestic non-bank lending boom.

That distinction is what separates this story from a Canadian credit story.

## Background: how private credit Canada differs from bank lending

Private credit means loans negotiated privately between a borrower and a non-bank lender — an investment fund, an insurer, an asset manager — rather than raised through a bank or a public bond issue. The loans are not traded on an exchange and disclosure obligations are far lighter.

The sector grew rapidly through the low-rate years and has continued expanding since. The Bank of Canada has been tracking it in its **Financial Stability Report**, which has flagged "complex structures, limited transparency and the fact that private credit is untested in a downturn" as reasons its behaviour is hard to predict.

The August article extends that work by measuring Canadian holdings specifically.

## Why private credit Canada figures matter to borrowers

For anyone borrowing in Canada, the honest answer is that this is **context, not consequence** — and the Bank's own numbers are the reason.

Its research describes private credit as "a stable and relatively limited source of funding" for Canadian businesses, with **non-bank loans accounting for roughly 15% of external business financing** over the past decade. **Banks and public debt markets together supply about three-quarters** of non-financial business funding. Domestic business borrowing still runs mainly through banks.

What the Bank does flag is a spillover channel. In its words: **"a sharp downturn in the performance of private credit abroad could affect Canadian investors and business lending in the domestic economy."** That is conditional and forward-looking, not a forecast.

Three reasons **private credit Canada** figures are still worth following:

- **Pension money is involved.** $215 billion of pension fund assets sit in this market, which makes it a retirement-savings question as much as a lending one.
- **The insurer concentration is high.** At roughly 22% of invested assets, the three largest life insurers are meaningfully exposed.
- **Transparency is the acknowledged weakness.** The Bank states that "transparency is limited, leverage can be difficult to measure, and links to the broader financial system are still being mapped."

The last point is why the research exists. Measuring the exposure is the first step in understanding what a downturn in it would do.

![private credit Canada — reviewing account and portfolio data, the kind of institutional exposure the Bank of Canada set out to measure](https://images.pexels.com/photos/28841475/pexels-photo-28841475.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Key takeaways

- Total Canadian exposure: **approximately $500 billion**, early 2026
- Pension funds **$215 billion** (~9% of invested assets); life insurers **just over $200 billion** (~22%)
- Investment funds **$54 billion**, up **more than 60% since 2020**, over two-fifths in real estate
- Bank lending to asset managers **at least $40 billion**, about **1%** of overall bank lending
- Most exposure is **outside Canada**, primarily in the United States
- Non-bank loans are roughly **15%** of Canadian external business financing
- The Bank's concern is **spillover and transparency**, not a domestic credit event

## Conclusion

The $500 billion private credit Canada headline is real, but it describes Canadian institutions lending into foreign markets rather than a shadow banking system taking over domestic credit. On the Bank of Canada's own measurements, Canadian businesses still borrow overwhelmingly from banks and bond markets, and private credit remains a limited domestic funding source.

The finding worth remembering is the one the Bank states plainly: this market is **untested in a downturn**, and the links between it and the rest of the financial system "are still being mapped." A $500 billion position in something not yet fully understood is a reasonable thing for a central bank to measure carefully — and for savers with pension money in it to keep an eye on.
