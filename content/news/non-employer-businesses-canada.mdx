Statistics Canada put the number of **non-employer businesses Canada** wide at **3.78 million** in its 14 August release, alongside 1.37 million employer businesses. It is a striking figure, and it is also an undercount by design — because a revenue threshold written into the methodology excludes an entire tier of Canadian business activity before the counting starts.

![Non-employer businesses Canada — business records and paperwork on a desk](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8441775/pexels-photo-8441775.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Non-Employer Businesses Canada: The Published Figures

- **3.78 million non-employer businesses** in June 2026, up from **3.59 million** a year earlier
- **1.37 million employer businesses**, down slightly from 1.38 million
- Only businesses with **annual revenues above $30,000** are included
- Data is semi-annual, covering **June and December**, published in August and February
- Source is the **Business Register**, compiled mainly from Canada Revenue Agency records

## What the $30,000 Threshold Leaves Out

This is the detail that changes how the headline should be read. To appear among non-employer businesses Canada records, a business must report annual revenues **greater than $30,000**.

Consider what sits below that line:

- A weekend trade earning $1,800 a month
- A first-year consultancy that started in March
- A seasonal operation running four months of the year
- Someone driving, delivering or renting out a room part-time
- Any venture that had a bad year

None of them appear. The 3.78 million figure is therefore best understood as a count of **established** small businesses, not a census of Canadians running something of their own. The real population is larger, and nobody publishes how much larger.

That matters for interpretation. When commentary treats this number as "the number of self-employed Canadians," it is quietly excluding the newest and smallest ventures — which are precisely the ones most likely to be growing, and most likely to need credit.

## Why the Counts and the Jobs Numbers Disagree

A second measurement issue is worth understanding, because the two datasets released this month tell different-sized stories.

| | What it counts | Latest |
|---|---|---|
| Business Register | Businesses filing with the CRA above $30,000 | 3.78 million non-employer |
| Labour Force Survey | People, classified by their **main** job | Self-employment +44,000 in July; +73,000 since April |

The register recorded roughly **190,000 more businesses** than a year earlier. The survey's self-employment growth over recent months is considerably smaller. Both can be accurate at once, because a person holding a salaried job while running a $50,000 business appears in one and not the other.

The most defensible reading is that a good share of the growth sits **alongside** employment. It is a reading supported by the structure of the two datasets rather than by any single figure, and it should be held loosely.

## Background: The Warning Attached to Non-Employer Businesses Canada Data

The agency attaches an unusually direct caution to this release. Period-to-period changes in the counts, it notes, warrant careful interpretation, because they can reflect administrative or methodological adjustments to the Business Register rather than real-world business formation or closure.

That warning deserves more prominence than it usually gets. The Business Register is maintained continuously; entities are added, merged, reclassified and removed as tax records change. A year-over-year movement of a few percent is inside the range where methodology can matter as much as economics.

The responsible way to use the data is directional. Non-employer businesses have grown and employer businesses have not — a pattern consistent across several releases now. The exact size of the gap in any single year is a softer claim.

For wider context in the same week, Statistics Canada reported June manufacturing sales of $78.8 billion, up 9.3% for the second quarter, and wholesale sales of $92.5 billion, up 9.0% year over year.

## Why This Matters

For the people behind the non-employer businesses Canada figure, these measurement questions have very practical consequences.

1. **Being under the threshold does not mean being unbankable.** Lenders assess bank transaction data over 60 to 90 days, not StatCan's revenue cut-off. A $24,000 business with regular deposits reads perfectly well.
2. **Statistical invisibility affects policy.** Support programmes are designed around the businesses that show up in the data. The smallest operations are the least represented in it.
3. **Income evidence is the constant.** Whatever the size, the requirement is the same: identifiable, recurring deposits over several months.
4. **Mixing accounts obscures the picture.** Business income paid into a personal account alongside everything else is harder for any assessment to read, whoever is doing the reading.

## Non-Employer Businesses Canada: Key Takeaways

- The published count reached **3.78 million**, up roughly **190,000** year over year
- The **$30,000 revenue threshold** excludes an unmeasured tier of smaller ventures
- Employer businesses **edged down** to 1.37 million over the same period
- The register's growth **exceeds** the self-employment growth in the Labour Force Survey
- Statistics Canada **warns** that period-to-period changes may reflect methodology, not the economy

## Conclusion

The figure for non-employer businesses Canada published this month is real, useful and incomplete — and knowing which parts are which is the difference between reading it well and repeating it badly. It counts established small businesses above a revenue line, from tax records, twice a year, with an explicit caution about comparing one period to the next.

Read that way, it still supports a clear conclusion: the payroll-employer share of Canadian business is drifting down, and the owner-operator share is drifting up.
